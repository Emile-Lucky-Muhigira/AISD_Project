\begin{abstract}
ElimuSentry is an early-warning system for school dropout. For a child aged 8--13 who is in school when a household survey visits, it estimates whether the child will be out of school at the next visit, about four years later, without having finished basic education. This report covers the step before any model is built: checking the data, finding and fixing problems, and deciding, with evidence, how every piece of information will be prepared. The data come from the Ghana Socioeconomic Panel Survey, which visited the same households in 2009/10, 2013/14 and 2017/18. About 330 separate survey files were joined into one row per child. Children first seen in 2009/10 are used to build the model (1,307 children, 14.4\% dropped out); children first seen in 2013/14 are kept aside to test it later (1,395 children, 10.4\% dropped out). Each child is described by 33 pieces of information (``features''). The main findings: almost every child in the model-building table has at least one missing answer (only 4.1\% are complete), most measurements are lopsided rather than bell-shaped, and three features repeat information already held by others. The plan corrects recording errors with fixed rules, fills gaps with a method chosen for the reason each gap exists, tames extreme values instead of deleting children, removes the three redundant features (33 $\rightarrow$ 30), converts categories into numbers sensibly, and tests the model on a later time period with safeguards that stop test information leaking into training.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction: Problem, Data and Repository}
% ===============================================================
\textbf{Problem.} School dropout in Sub-Saharan Africa usually happens gradually: absences pile up and learning falls behind before a child leaves \cite{balfanz2007}. The aim is to warn early, using information collected while the child is still in school (attendance, test results, household wealth, parents' education, school conditions), and to explain each warning in plain terms \cite{shap}. This supports Sustainable Development Goal 4 (targets 4.1 and 4.5).

\textbf{Data.} Ghana Socioeconomic Panel Survey, Waves 1--3 (2009/10, 2013/14, 2017/18), by ISSER (University of Ghana), Yale University and Northwestern University \cite{gsps}. The data arrive as about 330 separate files (one per questionnaire section per visit), linked by a household number and a person number.

\textbf{What is predicted, and what type of task this is.} \textbf{Dropout = 1} if the child is out of school at the next visit without having finished Junior Secondary School (JSS3); \textbf{0} if still in school. This is a \textbf{binary classification} task (two possible outcomes), and dropouts are the smaller group.

\textbf{Repository and reproducibility.} The GitHub repository follows the structure required by the assignment. Raw and processed data are kept out of GitHub by the \var{.gitignore} file (the raw download is 113\,MB, above GitHub's 100\,MB limit) and can be downloaded from the source link in the README. Two notebooks reproduce everything: \var{notebooks/01\_build\_dataset.ipynb} joins the survey files into the analysis tables, and \var{notebooks/02\_phase1\_audit.ipynb} produces every number, table and figure in this report (result tables are also saved in \var{phase1\_strategy/}). Software versions are fixed in \var{requirements.txt} (Python 3.11, pandas 3.0.6, scikit-learn 1.9.1, xgboost 3.2.0, pyreadstat 1.3.6).

\textbf{Reading this report.} The assignment requires several technical terms; each is explained in plain language in the Appendix at the end, just before the references. Sections II--IV follow the assignment's parts A, B and C.

\textbf{Problems found while building the table} (details in \var{notebooks/01\_build\_dataset.ipynb}): some files used an old Windows text format; most numbers were stored as text; codes changed between visits (e.g.\ sex was recorded as 1/2 in 2009/10 but 1/5 in 2013/14) and were made consistent; 2 duplicate people were removed; district numbers repeat across regions (112 different codes stand for 136 real districts in the model-building table and 137 in the testing table), so region and district were combined into one district code; test answers were stored as letters or codes rather than right/wrong, so they were marked with an answer key recovered from the 2017/18 survey (it matches the most common answer on every question); and the question on a parent's education was skipped when the parent lives in the same household, so that parent's own education record was used instead (apparent missing information on father's education fell from about 54\% to 7.7\%).

\subsection*{A3. Missing information before any filling}
This is the profile before any processing that changes real values: nothing has been filled, capped or deleted. The only step applied first is turning disguised gaps --- special codes such as ``99 = on holiday'' and physically impossible entries (Table~\ref{tab:corrections}) --- into ``missing'', because counting them as real values would hide true gaps. \textbf{(a) Per feature:} Table~\ref{tab:missing}.

\textbf{(b) Overall:} 3,820 of 43,131 answers (8.86\%) are missing in the model-building table; 1,826 of 46,035 (3.97\%) in the testing table. \textbf{(c) Complete rows:} only 53 of 1,307 children (4.1\%) have \emph{no} missing answer; 1,254 (95.9\%) have at least one. In the testing table, 467 of 1,395 (33.5\%) are complete. Ten features (12 in testing) have no gaps at all. Gaps are concentrated in five features (Fig.~\ref{fig:missing}). The two tables differ because the 2009/10 survey used paper forms, while the 2013/14 survey used tablets that required an answer.

\textbf{How the imbalance is handled.} Dropouts are the minority (about 1 in 7), so a ``model'' that simply says nobody drops out would already be right 85.6\% of the time while being useless. Accuracy is therefore not used to judge the model; instead we measure how well it ranks the real dropouts near the top of its list (PR-AUC) and how many dropouts are caught when the top 20\% riskiest children are flagged \cite{saito2015}. During training, each dropout is given about 6 times the weight of a child who stays in school (\emph{class weighting}: \var{scale\_pos\_weight} in XGBoost \cite{xgboost}, \var{class\_weight="balanced"} in logistic regression), recalculated within each fold. We do \emph{not} create copies or artificial dropouts (over-sampling, SMOTE): weighting gives the same balancing effect without adding made-up children, and each made-up child would be built by mixing real children, often siblings or neighbours, which could carry their information across the checks in C2. Because the extra weight pushes risk scores upwards, the risk percentages will be re-adjusted (calibrated) before use \cite{zadrozny2002}, using only 2009/10 predictions on the checking folds and never test outcomes. The lower dropout rate in the later period (10.4\% instead of 14.4\%) cannot be corrected without test outcomes, so it is reported as a limitation (Section~\ref{sec:limits}).

\subsection*{Fixing recording errors first}
\label{sec:corrections}
Values that are physically impossible, and special codes such as ``99 = on holiday'', are recording errors, not genuine extreme values. They were fixed with simple fixed rules that do not learn anything from the data, so they cannot leak information, and the same rules were applied to both tables (Table~\ref{tab:corrections}). After the travel-time fix, the two tables agree closely (average 31.8 vs 32.4 minutes), which supports the rule.

\textbf{Evidence.} For every feature with gaps we checked (i) whether children with a gap drop out more or less often than others (chi-square test), and (ii) whether gaps are more common for certain groups --- by age, sex, urban/rural, northern regions, wealth (key assets) or whether the mother lives at home (Mann--Whitney and chi-square tests, significant if $p<0.05$). Each feature is then assigned to one of the three mechanisms: gaps explained by something we can see (age, grade, region, the questionnaire's own skip rules) are \textbf{MAR}; gaps that probably depend on the hidden value itself are \textbf{MNAR}; gaps not linked to anything we can see are \emph{consistent with} \textbf{MCAR} (missing by chance). With about nine checks per feature, one weak link ($0.01\le p<0.05$) can arise by chance, so for features with fewer than 20 gaps it is not taken as MAR. No test can \emph{prove} that a gap is MCAR \cite{rubin1976, littlerubin}, but those features are less than 3\% missing, so any distortion is negligible. The result for every feature is in Table~\ref{tab:b1}.

\textbf{Rules for filling gaps}, applied the same way everywhere:
\begin{itemize}
\item Numbers that are roughly balanced ($|$skew$|<0.5$) $\rightarrow$ fill with the \textbf{average} (mean).
\item Numbers with a long tail ($|$skew$|\ge0.5$) $\rightarrow$ fill with the \textbf{middle value} (median), which a few extreme values cannot pull.
\item Yes/no items and ordered categories $\rightarrow$ fill with the \textbf{most common answer} (mode).
\item Labels whose gaps carry meaning $\rightarrow$ add a separate ``missing'' option.
\item When the gap itself tells us something (it follows from the questionnaire design, it describes the family situation, or it is linked to dropout), a yes/no \textbf{``was missing'' flag} is added so the model can use that fact --- unless there are fewer than 20 gaps (too few for a flag to be learned) or a rule recovers the true value (hours missed).
\item School grade $\rightarrow$ estimated from the 5 most similar children (\textbf{KNN}), because grade is closely tied to age and region. KNN is used only here: grade is the one feature that the others predict well, and for the rest a simple fill is more stable with 1,307 children.
\end{itemize}
All filling values are learned from the model-building part of each fold only (Section C2), never from the data used to check the model. The few gaps that appear only in the testing table (e.g.\ relationship to head, 3 children) are filled with the same rules, using values learned from model-building data.

\textbf{Why we do not simply delete children with gaps.} Only 4.1\% of children have no gap at all, so deleting incomplete rows would leave 53 children. It would also remove younger and northern children far more than others, exactly where the gaps concentrate.

\textbf{Children whose outcome is unknown.} Children who could not be found again are older and much less often live with their parents (father at home 52\% vs 66\%, mother 65\% vs 83\%, $p<10^{-11}$); grandchildren, foster children and other relatives were lost 54--59\% of the time, against 33\% for the head's own children. These are known risk factors for dropout, so the outcome is probably missing \emph{because} of a higher dropout risk (\textbf{MNAR}), and the true dropout rate is probably somewhat higher than measured. A missing outcome cannot be filled in; this is reported as a limitation (Section~\ref{sec:limits}).

\textbf{Which rule.} The \textbf{Z-score rule} flags a value when $|Z| = |(x-\mu)/\sigma| > 3$, i.e.\ more than 3 standard deviations from the average. It assumes bell-shaped data, but every numeric feature failed the D'Agostino--Pearson test for a bell shape ($p<0.001$), so it is shown in Table~\ref{tab:b2} for comparison only. The \textbf{IQR rule} \cite{tukey} is used instead: a value is flagged if it lies more than 1.5 times the width of the middle half of the data beyond the edges of that middle half ($[Q_1-1.5\,\text{IQR},\,Q_3+1.5\,\text{IQR}]$). For example, the middle half of children travel between 10 and 40 minutes (a range of 30 minutes), so the rule flags anything above $40+1.5\times30=85$ minutes. Applied to the raw values, this rule flags many genuine high values in long-tailed features (77 children travelling more than 85 minutes, 146 household-goods values). After a log transform, which compresses the long tail, it flags only a handful, so the limits are computed on the log scale (Table~\ref{tab:b2}).

Each feature is tested with the method that suits its type. \textbf{Numbers and ordered categories}: Mann--Whitney test, which lines all children up from lowest to highest value and checks whether dropouts sit higher or lower in the line (it works for lopsided data), with the \emph{rank-biserial} correlation as the strength of the link \cite{kerby}. \textbf{Yes/no items and labels}: chi-square test with \emph{Cram\'er's V} as the strength. \textbf{Every feature}: mutual information (MI), which captures any kind of relationship and is used to rank the features (Fig.~\ref{fig:importance}) \cite{kraskov, ross2014}. Pearson $r$ and point-biserial correlation were not used because they are pulled strongly by lopsided values and extremes, and none of our features is bell-shaped (B2); rank-biserial measures the same thing using rankings, so extreme values cannot distort it. ANOVA with a two-group target is the same as a $t$-test, which also assumes bell-shaped data; Mann--Whitney is its rank-based replacement. \textbf{Matching matrix} (target = binary): continuous, discrete and ordinal features $\rightarrow$ Mann--Whitney + rank-biserial $r$ + MI; nominal and binary features $\rightarrow$ chi-square + Cram\'er's $V$ + MI; feature-to-feature checks (B4b) $\rightarrow$ Spearman $\rho$ and VIF.

\textbf{How to read Table~\ref{tab:b4a}.} ``Type'' decides the ``Test'' (MW = Mann--Whitney, $\chi^2$ = chi-square). ``Strength of link'': 0 means no link, and the further from 0 the stronger (MW: $-1$ to $+1$, where $+$ means dropouts have \emph{higher} values and $-$ \emph{lower} values than children who stayed; $V$ for labels: 0 to 1). ``Could be chance?'' is the $p$-value: below 0.05 means the link is clear. MI is how much the feature tells us about dropout and is used to rank the rows. The last column says the same thing in plain words (typical = middle value).

\textbf{Procedure.} (1) Compare every pair of features (Spearman correlation, which suits lopsided data) and flag pairs with $|\rho|\ge0.85$. (2) Check how much each feature is already explained by all the others together (VIF) and flag values above 10 \cite{obrien2007}. (3) \textbf{Decision rule:} in each flagged pair or group, keep the feature with more information about dropout (higher MI in Table~\ref{tab:b4a}); if similar, keep the one measured directly over one calculated from others. (4) Re-check after removal. The result is in Table~\ref{tab:b4b} and Figs.~\ref{fig:corr}--\ref{fig:collinear}.

People per room has a low VIF (1.77) even though it closely follows rooms, because VIF only looks for straight-line overlap and this link is curved; the pair check, which works on rankings, catches it. The pair check finds the two calculated features; the VIF check finds the age--grade--over-age relationship that no single pair reveals (Fig.~\ref{fig:collinear}c). In theory school grade's VIF would be infinite (it is fully determined by age and over-age); it shows 19.9 only because its 81 missing values were filled with a typical value for this check. Removing these three features takes the count from 33 to 30 \textbf{without losing information}, since each one can be rebuilt from features that remain. Because the removal follows from how the features are built (each is calculated from others), the list of features to remove is fixed before cross-validation. For people per room and lost a parent, the removed feature is the one calculated from the others; in the age--grade--over-age group any one can be calculated from the other two, and school grade is removed because it carries the least information about dropout. In Phase 2 this choice is re-checked inside each fold to confirm that the same feature is dropped in all five folds, so it does not rely on the checking parts.

\textbf{Categories into numbers (encoding).} Labels with no order (region, school type, relationship to head, test taken?) have at most 10 options, so each option becomes its own yes/no column (\emph{one-hot encoding}); this adds few columns and does not pretend one option is ``bigger'' than another. Relationship to head is first grouped into three options (own child 84\%, grandchild, other) because the rare options are too small to learn from. Ordered categories (parents' education and textbooks; school grade is removed in B4b) are turned into ordered numbers 0, 1, 2\ldots{} (lowest = 0; e.g.\ education: none 0, primary 1, middle/JSS 2, secondary 3, post-secondary 4), which keeps their order without adding any columns (\emph{ordinal encoding}); ``has textbooks'' is re-numbered so that a higher number means more books. Yes/no items become 0/1. A more complex method (target encoding) is not needed and would risk leakage with only 1,307 children.

\textbf{Money and inflation.} The typical value of household goods is 2.56 times higher in the testing table (1,038 vs 405 cedis), mostly because of inflation between 2009 and 2013. Household-goods value and school spending are therefore turned into a \textbf{rank} between 0 (lowest) and 1 (highest), the usual survey practice for comparing wealth across rounds \cite{filmer2001}. Ranks have fixed limits, so this replaces the capping planned for these two features in B3. The ranking is learned only from model-building data: inside cross-validation from the building part of each fold, and for the final model from all 2009/10 children. Before 2013/14 values are ranked against this 2009/10 reference, they are converted to 2009/10 prices using Ghana's official consumer price index, so no information from the test children is used. As a check, ranking within the 2013/14 survey itself will also be reported.

\textbf{Common scale (scaling).} The main model, XGBoost, is built from decision trees that only ask questions such as ``is travel time above 45 minutes?''. The answer depends only on the \emph{order} of values, so changing the units changes nothing, and \textbf{no rescaling is needed}. The simpler comparison model (regularised logistic regression, which shrinks every feature's effect by the same rule, so features on bigger scales would be treated unfairly) and the dimension-reduction summary planned for Phase 2 (PCA) combine the numbers directly, so features with large values (e.g.\ minutes of travel) would swamp features with small ones (e.g.\ a 0--7 score). For these, features with a long tail of high values are first log-transformed (except days teacher absent, where 75\% of values are 0) and then all numeric features are put on a common scale (\emph{standardised} with scikit-learn's \var{StandardScaler}: average 0, spread 1). Standardising works best on balanced shapes; the log is what makes the long-tailed features balanced enough for it (Fig.~\ref{fig:transform}). Standardising is preferred over squeezing values into 0--1 (\var{MinMaxScaler}), because Min--Max is thrown off by extreme values and none of our models (no neural network) requires a 0--1 range. The two money ranks are already bounded between 0 and 1; for the simple model they are standardised like the other numbers so that all inputs share one scale. The full plan is in Table~\ref{tab:c1}, and Fig.~\ref{fig:transform} shows the steps on one feature.

\textbf{Final width:} 25 single columns (16 numbers or ordered numbers + 9 yes/no items) + 24 yes/no columns from the label options + 4 ``was missing'' flags (B1) = \textbf{53 columns} for XGBoost; 48 for logistic regression (one option per label is left out as the reference).

\textbf{(a) How the data are split.} The split is \textbf{by time}, not at random: the model is built on children first seen in 2009/10 (1,307 child records, 48\% of the 2,702 usable records) and tested \emph{once} on children first seen in 2013/14 (1,395 records, 52\%). Within the model-building table, 5-fold cross-validation (about 80\% build / 20\% check in each round) is used to tune the model. With only 188 dropouts, a random 70/15/15 split would leave about 28 dropouts in each of the checking and testing parts --- too few for a reliable result --- whereas cross-validation lets every child be used for both building and checking.

\textbf{(b) Same dropout share in every fold (stratification).} Folds are stratified, so each keeps about 14\% dropouts (Table~\ref{tab:folds}). The testing table cannot be stratified because it is fixed by time; its lower dropout rate (10.4\%) is reported openly. Risk percentages are calibrated only on 2009/10 checking-fold predictions; no test outcome is used for any adjustment.

\textbf{(c) Keeping related children together (grouping).} Brothers and sisters share a household (552 of the 1,307 children live with another child in the sample), and children in the same district share schools and local conditions. Folds are therefore formed by \textbf{whole districts} (\var{StratifiedGroupKFold}); since every household lies inside one district, siblings are never split between building and checking either.

\textbf{(d) Time order.} Outcomes for model building are observed in 2013/14 and testing outcomes in 2017/18, so the model is always tested on a later period and no information from the future is used. Inside the model-building table every child comes from the same visit, so there is no time order to respect there, and ordinary (stratified, grouped) folds are correct; a rolling \var{TimeSeriesSplit} would only apply if there were many time points. 220 children were in school at both 2009/10 and 2013/14 and appear in both tables. Their 2013/14 status is already known when the 2017/18 prediction is made, so this is not leakage from the future; but the same child should not be on both sides of the final test. The \textbf{main test result therefore excludes these 220 children} (1,175 testing records); the full testing table is reported only as a check, as a working system would re-check a child every year.

\textbf{(e) Tuning without leakage.} Model settings, the adjustment of risk percentages (calibration) and the cut-off for flagging the riskiest 20\% are chosen using only predictions on the checking part of each fold. Every step that learns from data (filling values, capping limits, encoders, scaler) is placed in one scikit-learn \var{Pipeline} built around a \var{ColumnTransformer} \cite{sklearn}. In each fold it is \emph{fitted} (\var{fit}) on the building part only (\var{X\_train}) and then only \emph{applied} (\var{transform}) to the checking part; at the end it is fitted once on all 2009/10 children and only applied to the test children. Information therefore never passes from the checking or test data into building \cite{kaufman2012}.

\textbf{Order of steps that prevents leakage:}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Fix recording errors} (Table~\ref{tab:corrections}): fixed rules, nothing learned; applied to all data.
\item \textbf{Convert money values to 2009/10 prices} using the official consumer price index: a fixed rule, nothing learned from the data.
\item \textbf{Inside each fold, learned from the building part only:} fill gaps (B1; school grade is filled first so that years over-age can be recalculated) $\rightarrow$ add ``was missing'' flags $\rightarrow$ drop the three repeating features (B4b) $\rightarrow$ rank the money values against the building part (C1) $\rightarrow$ log transform and cap at limits from the building part (B3) $\rightarrow$ turn categories into numbers (C1) $\rightarrow$ common scale (simple model only) $\rightarrow$ train the model with extra weight on dropouts (A4).
\item \textbf{Choose model settings}, calibration and flagging cut-off from the checking parts.
\item \textbf{Build the final model} on all 2009/10 children, then test it \textbf{once} on the 2013/14 children (main result without the 220 children who appear in both tables). The testing table influences no decision.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Changes over Time and Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}
% ===============================================================
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Children who could not be found again (MNAR risk).} They share known dropout risk factors (older, not living with parents), so the true dropout rate is probably understated. The 164 children rejected by the identity check are also poorer and more rural; allowing an age change of 1--8 years would add 59 children (13 of whom left school); this is tested as a sensitivity check.
\item \textbf{The situation changed between periods.} Dropout fell from 14.4\% to 10.4\% overall, from 28\% to 12\% in the Northern region and from 46\% to 12\% in Upper West. What the model learns about regions may not carry over, and its risk percentages may overstate later risk.
\item \textbf{Different ways of collecting data.} Paper forms (2009/10) and tablets (2013/14) leave different gaps (e.g.\ hours missed: 77.8\% vs 11.7\% missing), and the 2013/14 tests added a fifth answer option (``none of the above''), so scores are not perfectly comparable. A ``was missing'' flag can therefore mean different things in the two periods, so a rule replaces a flag where possible (e.g.\ blank hours missed = 0, Table~\ref{tab:b1}); the effect of the four kept flags will be compared between periods in Phase 2.
\item \textbf{Change from the proposal.} Household consumption totals exist only for 2009/10, so wealth is measured in both periods by key assets, value of household goods, electricity, rooms and household size.
\item \textbf{Links, not causes.} The tests show which features go together with dropout, which is what prediction needs; they do not show that these features cause dropout.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Appendix: Plain-Language Guide to the Technical Terms}
\label{app:glossary}
The assignment requires several technical terms. Each is explained below in plain words.

{\footnotesize
\noindent\textbf{Feature:} One piece of information about a child, e.g.\ age or hours of class missed.\par\vspace{2pt}
\noindent\textbf{Target ($Y$):} What we want to predict: dropped out (1) or still in school (0).\par\vspace{2pt}
\noindent\textbf{Model-building table (training data) / testing table (test data):} Children used to build the model / children kept aside to check it honestly afterwards. The \emph{building part} and \emph{checking part} are the two pieces of the model-building table in each round of cross-validation.\par\vspace{2pt}
\noindent\textbf{Cross-validation (CV), fold:} Splitting the training data into 5 parts (``folds''); the model is built on 4 and checked on the 5th, rotating 5 times.\par\vspace{2pt}
\noindent\textbf{Leakage:} When information from the data used for checking sneaks into building the model, making results look better than they really are.\par\vspace{2pt}
\noindent\textbf{MCAR / MAR / MNAR:} Three reasons data go missing: \emph{MCAR} --- by pure chance; \emph{MAR} --- for a reason we can see in other answers (e.g.\ younger children were not tested); \emph{MNAR} --- because of the hidden value itself (e.g.\ a child who cannot read leaves the reading test blank).\par\vspace{2pt}
\noindent\textbf{Imputation:} Filling a missing value with a sensible estimate.\par\vspace{2pt}
\noindent\textbf{Mean / median / mode:} The average / the middle value / the most common answer.\par\vspace{2pt}
\noindent\textbf{Typical value:} The middle value (median): half of the children are below it, half above.\par\vspace{2pt}
\noindent\textbf{Skewness (skew):} How lopsided a distribution is. Near 0 = balanced; large = a long tail of very high values (e.g.\ a few very rich households).\par\vspace{2pt}
\noindent\textbf{Outlier; IQR rule; Z-score rule:} An unusually extreme value; two standard rules for spotting one: the \emph{IQR rule} flags values far outside the middle half of the data, the \emph{Z-score rule} flags values more than 3 standard deviations from the average (only valid for bell-shaped data).\par\vspace{2pt}
\noindent\textbf{Capping (Winsorising):} Pulling an extreme value back to a set limit instead of deleting the child.\par\vspace{2pt}
\noindent\textbf{Log transform (log1p):} A standard way to compress a long tail so that very large values no longer dominate.\par\vspace{2pt}
\noindent\textbf{$p$-value:} The chance of seeing a link this strong if there were really no link. Below 0.05 is called ``significant''.\par\vspace{2pt}
\noindent\textbf{Effect size (``strength of link'' in the tables):} How \emph{strong} a link is (0 = none, larger = stronger), regardless of sample size. Two are used: \emph{rank-biserial $r$} for numbers ($-1$ to $+1$; the sign says whether dropouts have higher or lower values) and \emph{Cram\'er's $V$} for categories (0 to 1).\par\vspace{2pt}
\noindent\textbf{Mann--Whitney test; chi-square test:} Two standard tests of whether dropouts differ from children who stayed. \emph{Mann--Whitney}: lines all children up from lowest to highest value and checks whether dropouts sit higher or lower in the line. \emph{Chi-square}: checks whether the dropout rate differs between groups (e.g.\ urban vs rural).\par\vspace{2pt}
\noindent\textbf{Mutual information (MI):} How much knowing a feature reduces uncertainty about dropout; captures any kind of relationship.\par\vspace{2pt}
\noindent\textbf{Correlation ($\rho$); VIF:} How closely two features move together ($-1$ to $+1$); VIF (variance inflation factor) shows how much a feature is already explained by all the others together (above 10 = redundant).\par\vspace{2pt}
\noindent\textbf{One-hot encoding:} Turning a category with several options (e.g.\ 10 regions) into separate yes/no columns.\par\vspace{2pt}
\noindent\textbf{Scaling (standardising):} Putting numbers measured in different units (cedis, hours, years) on a common scale.\par\vspace{2pt}
\noindent\textbf{XGBoost; logistic regression:} The main prediction model (built from many decision trees); a simpler baseline model used for comparison.\par\vspace{2pt}
\noindent\textbf{PR-AUC:} A score from 0 to 1 for how well the model puts real dropouts at the top of its risk list; it is the right score when dropouts are rare.\par\vspace{2pt}
\noindent\textbf{PCA:} A method that summarises many features into a few combined ones; planned for Phase 2.\par\vspace{2pt}
\noindent\textbf{KNN (5 nearest neighbours):} Filling a gap using the values of the 5 children who are most similar on other features (age, sex, urban/rural, region, assets).\par\vspace{2pt}
\noindent\textbf{Stratification; grouping:} Making every fold keep the same share of dropouts (about 14\%); keeping related children (same household or district) together in one fold.\par\vspace{2pt}
\noindent\textbf{StratifiedGroupKFold; \var{X\_train}; Pipeline:} The scikit-learn tool that makes folds with the same dropout share and whole districts; the building part of the data; the container that re-learns every preparation step inside each fold.\par\vspace{2pt}
\noindent\textbf{Cedi; P3, JSS:} Ghana's currency; Primary year 3 and Junior Secondary School (the last three years of basic education).\par\vspace{2pt}
\noindent\textbf{Overfitting; statistical power:} A model learning quirks of its building data that do not repeat in new data; the ability of a study to detect a real link, which falls when there are fewer cases.\par\vspace{2pt}
\noindent\textbf{Class weighting:} Counting each dropout several times more during training so the model pays enough attention to the rare group (\var{scale\_pos\_weight} in XGBoost).\par\vspace{2pt}
\noindent\textbf{Calibration:} Adjusting risk scores so that ``20\% risk'' really means about 1 in 5 such children drop out.\par\vspace{2pt}
\noindent\textbf{D'Agostino--Pearson test:} A standard test of whether numbers follow a bell shape.\par\vspace{2pt}
\noindent\textbf{Target encoding:} Replacing each category with the dropout rate of that category; not used here because it can leak outcome information.\par\vspace{2pt}
\noindent\textbf{Regularised model:} A model that is penalised for relying too heavily on any one feature, which keeps it simple.\par\vspace{2pt}
\noindent\textbf{ColumnTransformer; fit / transform:} The scikit-learn tool that applies the right preparation to each group of columns; \emph{fit} = learn the settings (e.g.\ a median) from data, \emph{transform} = apply them.\par\vspace{2pt}
\noindent\textbf{TimeSeriesSplit:} A way of making folds that always checks on later time points; needed only when data have many time points.\par\vspace{2pt}
\noindent\textbf{Sensitivity check:} Repeating an analysis with a different reasonable choice to see whether the conclusion changes.\par\vspace{2pt}
\noindent\textbf{Consumer price index:} The official measure of how prices rise over time, used to convert money to the prices of one year.\par\vspace{2pt}
}
