\begin{abstract}
Phase 1 planned, with evidence, how every piece of information about a child in the Ghana Socioeconomic Panel Survey would be prepared for a school-dropout early-warning model. This report covers Phase 2: turning that plan into working code and checking it. The plan was built as one scikit-learn \var{Pipeline} around a \var{ColumnTransformer}, with custom preparation steps in \path{src/transformers.py}. It is fitted on the model-building children only (X\_train, 1,307 children from 2009/10) and then applied unchanged to the testing children (X\_test, 1,395 children from 2013/14). The output has exactly the planned size: 33 features become 30, and then 53 numeric columns for XGBoost or 48 for logistic regression, with no missing value left. The three removed features can be rebuilt exactly from the kept ones (0 differences in 3,921 checks), so the structural removals add no extra loss beyond the retained source fields. Eight diagnostic figures confirm each step. Most decisions were carried out as planned; five points changed or were sharpened by what the data showed. A retained fixed CPI adjustment aligns nominal monetary units; other cross-period changes remain descriptive. MI-only feature selection is unstable across folds; a fixed structural/domain rule determines the removals. The hours-missed rule is compared descriptively with median filling; no equivalence claim is made. All four ``was missing'' flags are less frequent later, by different proportions; survey mode alone does not establish the cause. And rank correlation was needed because the ordinary correlation would have missed one repeating pair.
\end{abstract}

data preprocessing, scikit-learn pipeline, leakage prevention, visual diagnostics, school dropout, Ghana panel survey

\section{Introduction}
% ===============================================================
\textbf{Aim of this phase.} Phase 1 used the Ghana Socioeconomic Panel Survey \cite{gsps} and decided how to fill gaps, treat extreme values, remove repeating features, encode categories, rescale numbers and split the data with fold-fitted preprocessing \cite{phase1}. Phase 2 has three tasks, which follow the assignment: (1) implement that strategy as fully runnable code with a strict X\_train / X\_test separation; (2) check every assumption with eight diagnostic figures; and (3) record where reality agreed with the plan and where it forced a change.

\textbf{What is in the repository.} \path{src/transformers.py} holds the custom preparation steps; \path{src/pipeline_builder.py} assembles them into the pipeline; \path{phase2_implementation/notebooks/pipeline_execution.ipynb} runs everything from start to finish, saves the eight figures to \path{figures/} at 300 DPI, writes the processed tables to \path{data/processed/} and the decision log to \path{phase2_implementation/decision_log.csv}. The notebook was run from top to bottom without errors.

\textbf{Scope.} Phase 2 is about preparing the data. A quick five-fold run of two models is included only to prove that the full pipeline (preparation plus model) works end to end with fold-fitted preprocessing. Tuning the models, adjusting the risk percentages (calibration) and the single final test belong to the modelling phase, so testing outcomes are not used for fitting, tuning, calibration or threshold selection. Aggregate temporal outcomes were inspected descriptively, so confirmatory independence is not claimed.

\textbf{Reading this report.} Technical terms are explained in plain language in the Appendix. Section~II describes what was built, Section~III the figures, Section~IV the decision log, Section~V the changes and their reasons, and Section~VI the checks on the output.

\textbf{One pipeline, two versions.} The pipeline is built with scikit-learn \cite{sklearn}. The same steps produce two versions of the data (Table~\ref{tab:steps}). The \textbf{XGBoost version} \cite{xgboost} keeps numbers in their own units, because a decision tree only compares values; it has 53 columns. The \textbf{logistic regression and PCA version} adds the log on long-tailed features and standardises the 16 numeric/ordinal columns, retains 32 indicator columns on their 0/1 scales, and leaves out one option per label as the reference; it has 48 columns. This follows Phase 1, C1 exactly.

\textbf{How leakage is prevented.} Every step that learns something (averages, middle values, neighbours, cap limits, money ranks, the list of options, the scaler) is inside the pipeline. The pipeline is \emph{fitted} on X\_train only and then only \emph{applied} to X\_test. Inside cross-validation it is fitted again on the building part of each fold. The notebook checks this directly: it saves the fitted pipeline, applies it to X\_test, and confirms that nothing it learned has changed \cite{kaufman2012}. The only step applied outside the pipeline, the inflation adjustment, uses a fixed published number, not the data.

\textbf{The split.} Inputs contain 1,307 training and 1,395 later-period children. Primary temporal evaluation is restricted to the 731 children in 605 new households (76 dropouts), with no shared household or person. Secondary evaluation uses 1,175 new people but allows 370 shared households. Five training folds keep whole districts together. Aggregate later-period covariates and outcomes were already explored, so this is retrospective temporal evaluation, not an untouched external confirmation. These subsets are fixed by IDs, not their outcomes.

\textbf{The inflation factor.} Phase 1 promised to convert 2013/14 money to 2009/10 prices using Ghana's consumer price index. The interview dates in the raw survey files show that the first visit took place from November 2009 to April 2010 and the second, labelled 2013/14, mostly from March to October 2014, with a few households in 2015. Using World Bank annual consumer price inflation for Ghana \cite{wbcpi} (index 2009 = 100: 2010 = 110.7, 2014 = 172.6, 2015 = 202.3), weighted by the number of interviews in each year, the price level was 107.5 at the first visit and 174.0 at the second. Every 2013/14 amount is therefore divided by $174.0/107.5 = 1.62$.

\section{Deviations and Rationale}
% ===============================================================
\subsection{Price adjustment and rank interpretation}
Retain the fixed external CPI conversion (factor 1.62) and learn the rank reference on training rows only. Training-reference ranks bound long-tailed monetary inputs without fitting the later distribution. Previously explored later-period ranks differ from the training median, but that alone cannot identify a causal price or wealth change: composition, measurement and attrition can contribute. No claim is made that inflation explains most of the change or that school policy caused the spending difference. Within-wave test ranking is not used to fit the primary procedure or to choose it from test scores.

\subsection{Which repeating feature to drop is not stable across folds}

The removal list is fixed from feature construction and domain meaning, not from full-training dropout associations. Retain household size and rooms instead of derived crowding; retain parent-alive source fields instead of derived orphan status; retain age and the domain-relevant over-age signal instead of grade. The separate MI-only diagnostic agrees in 1 of 5 folds (Table~\ref{tab:folds}), so it is not represented as a stable selection algorithm. This diagnostic does not change the fixed list. Source identities are checked on all training records, including matching missing values; this is a check of structural removal, not a claim that all preprocessing is lossless.

\subsection{Hours missed: an assumption with a sensitivity comparison}
The retained rule assumes zero missed hours when attendance is recorded; it is not recovery of a known survey answer. It sets 756 blanks to zero and leaves 261 holiday/unknown-attendance cases for the later median fill. The untuned five-fold comparison gives logistic-regression PR-AUC 0.266 for the rule versus 0.275 for median filling, and XGBoost 0.252 versus 0.257. These are descriptive scores with substantial fold variation; no formal equivalence or no-harm conclusion is supported. The sensitivity is retained so a later training-only nested modelling comparison can assess the choice. Temporal test scores must not select it.

\subsection{Missing indicators can change meaning across periods}
Flags are on less often in the later cohort: teacher absent 56.8\% vs 31.8\%, hours attended 21.8\% vs 11.7\%, father's education 7.7\% vs 5.4\%, mother's education 3.8\% vs 1.9\%. Survey mode, coding and cohort composition may all matter; tablet use alone is not established as the cause. Retain the four design/family-context flags as a fixed modelling choice, not because an unadjusted significance test selected them. Compare their behaviour descriptively across periods and retain the uncertainty in transport claims.

\subsection{Rank correlation was needed, not the ordinary one}
The assignment's screening rule is written as $|r|\ge0.85$. To capture monotone curved relationships and reduce sensitivity to extreme values, Phase 1 used the rank (Spearman) correlation $\rho$ with the same threshold. Phase 2 shows why this mattered: for rooms and people per room, $\rho=-0.855$ but the ordinary (Pearson) correlation is only $r=-0.563$, because the relationship is curved (people per room = household size / rooms). With Pearson $r$, this repeating pair would have been missed, and the VIF check also misses it (VIF 1.77). For father alive and lost a parent, both measures give $-0.850$.

\textbf{Final size.} Table~\ref{tab:dims} confirms that the pipeline produces exactly the size planned in Phase 1: 33 features become 30, then 53 columns for XGBoost and 48 for logistic regression, for every child of X\_train and X\_test, with no missing value and no child deleted.

\textbf{Feature count reduced without information loss.} The assignment asks for proof that the reduction from 33 to 30 loses no information. \textbf{No leakage.} (1) Every learned setting is fitted on X\_train only; the notebook confirms that applying the pipeline to X\_test leaves its fitted settings unchanged. (2) In cross-validation the whole pipeline is re-fitted on the building part of each fold; no district or household is shared between building and checking parts. (3) Later outcomes are not used for fitting or selection. Prior descriptive inspection is disclosed; primary households and people are disjoint.

These flags are not model features.

\section{Limitations and Next Steps}
% ===============================================================
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0pt}
\item \textbf{Change over time.} Dropout fell from 14.4\% to 10.4\%, regional patterns changed, household goods rose in real terms and school spending fell. A model built on 2009/10 may overstate risk in the later period; this will be measured on the single final test.
\item \textbf{Flags across periods.} All four ``was missing'' flags are less frequent later, by different proportions (Section~V-D); their weight in the model will be compared between periods.
\item \textbf{Children who could not be found again.} As reported in Phase 1, they differ on observed characteristics, so selection bias is possible; neither the missingness mechanism nor the direction of dropout-rate bias is identified; the sensitivity check on the identity rule (age change of 1--8 years) is part of the modelling phase.
\item \textbf{Next steps (modelling phase).} Tune both models inside the same folds, adjust the risk percentages (calibration) on the checking parts, choose the cut-off for flagging the riskiest 20\%, and then freeze the procedure, then report primary new-household and secondary new-person temporal metrics. Seek a new external/future cohort for confirmation.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Appendix: Plain-Language Guide to the Technical Terms}
The terms below are used in this report; terms already explained in the Phase 1 report keep the same meaning.

{\footnotesize
\noindent\textbf{Pipeline:} A chain of preparation steps followed by a model, run in a fixed order. Fitting it learns every setting from the training data at once.\par\vspace{2pt}
\noindent\textbf{ColumnTransformer:} The part of the pipeline that applies the right preparation to each group of columns side by side (for example, a middle-value fill for lopsided numbers and one-hot encoding for labels).\par\vspace{2pt}
\noindent\textbf{Fit / transform:} \emph{Fit} = learn the settings (e.g.\ a middle value or a cap limit) from data; \emph{transform} = apply the stored settings to any data without learning anything new.\par\vspace{2pt}
\noindent\textbf{X\_train / X\_test:} The children used to build the model (2009/10) and the children kept aside to test it (2013/14).\par\vspace{2pt}
\noindent\textbf{Leakage:} When information from the data used for checking sneaks into building the model, making results look better than they really are.\par\vspace{2pt}
\noindent\textbf{Fold, building part, checking part:} Cross-validation splits X\_train into 5 parts; the model is built on 4 (the building part) and checked on the 5th (the checking part), rotating 5 times.\par\vspace{2pt}
\noindent\textbf{Custom transformer:} A preparation step written for this project (in \path{src/transformers.py}) that follows the same fit / transform rules as the standard scikit-learn steps.\par\vspace{2pt}
\noindent\textbf{KNN (nearest neighbours) filling:} Filling a missing grade from similar training children with numerical differences and category matches treated separately; neighbour count selected within training.\par\vspace{2pt}
\noindent\textbf{Capping:} Pulling an extreme value down to a set limit instead of deleting the child.\par\vspace{2pt}
\noindent\textbf{Rank (0--1):} The position of a value among the training values: 0 = lowest, 0.5 = middle, 1 = highest.\par\vspace{2pt}
\noindent\textbf{Consumer price index, inflation factor:} The official measure of how prices rise; dividing 2013/14 amounts by 1.62 expresses them in 2009/10 prices.\par\vspace{2pt}
\noindent\textbf{One-hot encoding:} Turning a label with several options (e.g.\ 10 regions) into separate yes/no columns.\par\vspace{2pt}
\noindent\textbf{Standardising:} Putting numbers on a common scale with average 0 and spread 1.\par\vspace{2pt}
\noindent\textbf{Correlation ($r$, $\rho$):} How closely two features move together, from $-1$ to $+1$. The ordinary (Pearson) $r$ only sees straight-line relationships; the rank (Spearman) $\rho$ also sees curved ones.\par\vspace{2pt}
\noindent\textbf{VIF:} How much a feature is already explained by all the others together (above 10 = redundant).\par\vspace{2pt}
\noindent\textbf{Mutual information:} How much knowing a feature reduces uncertainty about dropout; it captures any kind of relationship.\par\vspace{2pt}
\noindent\textbf{PCA, component, scree plot:} PCA combines many columns into a few new ones (components) that keep as much of the variation as possible. A scree plot shows how much variation each component keeps, and the cumulative line how much the first $k$ components keep together.\par\vspace{2pt}
\noindent\textbf{Class weighting:} Counting each dropout several times more during training so the model pays enough attention to the rare group.\par\vspace{2pt}
\noindent\textbf{PR-AUC, ROC-AUC:} Scores from 0 to 1 for how well a model puts real dropouts at the top of its risk list. For PR-AUC a model with no skill scores the dropout rate (0.144); for ROC-AUC it scores 0.5.\par\vspace{2pt}
\noindent\textbf{Calibration:} Adjusting risk scores so that ``20\% risk'' really means about 1 in 5 such children drop out.\par\vspace{2pt}
\noindent\textbf{Tie-break rule:} The Phase 1 rule used when two repeating features carry similar information: keep the one measured directly and remove the one calculated from others.\par\vspace{2pt}
}
